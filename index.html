Are we approaching the limit of semiconductor manufacturing?

This site presents a class research project on chip fabrication: a brief introduction, a historical timeline, and an analysis of current limits, supported by an annotated bibliography.

Summary. Since the transistor in 1947, chip technology has pursued smaller and more densely packed features. We are nearing atomic-scale constraints for silicon, yet Samuel K. Moore (2020, IEEE Spectrum, the magazine of the Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers) argues that industry “node” labels (such as 7 nm or 5 nm) no longer reflect true physical dimensions and can obscure how slowly progress has become. Woods (2021, MIT’s Computer Science and Artificial Intelligence Laboratory, or CSAIL) and Gallagher et al. (2024) highlight energy use and sustainability in lithography. Deloitte (2025) documents economic pressure. Hammer (2020, Center for Strategic and International Studies, or CSIS) shows that control over manufacturing equipment—especially extreme ultraviolet (EUV) lithography tools—matters as much as physics. The “limit,” therefore, is not a single measurement but the intersection of physics, power, cost, policy, and equipment access.

What is a semiconductor?

A semiconductor conducts electricity more readily than an insulator (for example, rubber) but less readily than a metal (for example, copper). Engineers introduce trace impurities through a process called doping to control where current flows. That control makes it possible to build transistors and integrated circuits (ICs). Łukasiak and Jakubowski (2010) provide a longer historical survey; the timeline on this site offers a shorter overview.

Core concepts

Energy bands. Electrons occupy discrete energy levels grouped into bands. Silicon is widely used because its band gap allows reliable switching at room temperature.

Transistor. A transistor acts as a tiny switch: a voltage applied to one terminal controls current between two others. Billions of transistors on one chip form a processor.

Chip fabrication. Factories use lithography—patterning material with light—to print circuits onto silicon wafers. Smaller features require advanced tools such as extreme ultraviolet (EUV) lithography, which uses very short-wavelength light and is costly and energy-intensive.
