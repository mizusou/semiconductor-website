Annotated bibliography

Research question: Are we approaching the limit of semiconductor manufacturing technology?

Synthesis. Since the transistor (1947), semiconductor technology has pursued smaller features and higher density. Although silicon approaches atomic-scale boundaries, Moore (2020) argues that the industry has shifted toward marketing-oriented node names that understate how slowly progress has become. Physical slowdowns interact with high energy demand (Woods, 2021; Gallagher et al., 2024), rising costs (Deloitte, 2025), and dependence on complex equipment—especially extreme ultraviolet (EUV) lithography machines (Hammer, 2020).

Sources

  1. Hammer, A. (2020). Exporting U.S. Innovative Capacity to China?: A Case Study of Semiconductor Manufacturing Equipment. In S. Kennedy (Ed.), China’s Uneven High Tech Drive: Implications for the United States (pp. 37–41). Center for Strategic and International Studies (CSIS).
    https://www.jstor.org/stable/resrep22605.13

    Accessed initially through an institutional library link (Elon University login required for off-campus use).

    Annotation: Hammer examines limits from a geopolitical perspective: who owns fabrication equipment. Physical chip limits are linked to control of manufacturing tools, especially extreme ultraviolet (EUV) lithography. Leadership in innovation depends on the equipment supply chain, not only on chip design. Useful for connecting technical detail with policy and economic barriers.

  2. Gallagher, E., Ragnarsson, L. Å., & Rolin, C. (2024). Sustainable semiconductor manufacturing: The role of lithography. IEEE Transactions on Semiconductor Manufacturing, 37(4), 440–444.
    https://doi.org/10.1109/TSM.2024.3416830

    Annotation: The authors emphasize sustainability as a limit often overlooked in purely technical discussions. Lithography cost rises as nodes shrink; indefinite scaling may be unrealistic. The question is not only whether a 2 nm chip is feasible, but whether production can be sustained without excessive energy use or environmental harm. A recent perspective beyond traditional physics-focused accounts.

  3. Łukasiak, L., & Jakubowski, A. (2010). History of Semiconductors. Journal of Telecommunications and Information Technology, 39(1), 3–9.
    https://doi.org/10.26636/jtit.2010.1.1015

    Annotation: A review paper with a broad historical overview, extensive references, and manufacturing detail. Dated relative to current scaling debates but valuable as background before reading newer sources.

  4. Deloitte. (2025, February 3). 2025 Global Semiconductor Industry Outlook. Deloitte Insights.
    https://www.deloitte.com/us/en/insights/industry/technology/technology-media-telecom-outlooks/semiconductor-industry-outlook.html

    Annotation: An industry outlook covering economic conditions. Semiconductors remain highly valuable; the market has grown, particularly with artificial intelligence (AI) workloads. Deloitte is an established firm and cites additional data sources.

  5. Moore, S. K. (2020, August). The node is nonsense. IEEE Spectrum, 57(8), 24–30.
    https://doi.org/10.1109/MSPEC.2020.9150552

    Annotation: Moore (not Gordon Moore) contends that node names such as 7 nm or 5 nm no longer reflect physical dimensions. Moore’s-law-style scaling has weakened; nanometer labels often function as marketing. Transistors are not approaching a literal 1 nm width, and two-dimensional measures poorly describe three-dimensional structures. Questions the simplified narrative of hitting a wall at 1 nm. Published in a reputable trade journal.

  6. Woods, A. (2021). The Death of Moore’s Law: What it means and what might fill the gap going forward. CSAIL Alliances, MIT.
    https://cap.csail.mit.edu/death-moores-law-what-it-means-and-what-might-fill-gap-going-forward

    Annotation: Woods outlines manufacturing challenges and argues that physical limits are near. She introduces Moore’s law, then discusses thermodynamic and size constraints on endless shrink. She also covers alternatives, including software efficiency: better code could reduce the need for ever-faster hardware. Source affiliated with MIT; useful for reframing “final limits” as obstacles that may be addressed through multiple paths.
