Limits on semiconductor manufacturing

This page addresses the research question: Are we approaching the limit of semiconductor manufacturing technology? The sources reviewed here suggest that there is no single barrier. Instead, progress is constrained by physical scaling, marketing-oriented naming, energy and sustainability, economics, and control over advanced equipment—especially extreme ultraviolet (EUV) lithography.

Node names and marketing. Samuel K. Moore (2020), writing in IEEE Spectrum (from the Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers), argues that labels such as 7 nm or 5 nm no longer correspond to a straightforward physical measurement on the chip. Scaling has slowed; the numbers serve partly as marketing. Transistors are not literally one nanometer wide, and a single two-dimensional length is a poor description of three-dimensional structures. The industry narrative is therefore not simply “we will reach 1 nm and stop.”

Physical constraints and Moore’s law

Woods (2021), writing for MIT’s Computer Science and Artificial Intelligence Laboratory (CSAIL), describes Moore’s law as the tendency for transistor counts to double approximately every two years, then explains how shrinking size and thermodynamics limit endless miniaturization. She also discusses alternatives, including software efficiency: if programs used computing resources more effectively, demand for raw processing power might grow more slowly. That reframes the limit as partly addressable without relying only on smaller transistors.

Energy, lithography, and sustainability

Gallagher, Ragnarsson, and Rolin (2024) examine sustainable manufacturing with emphasis on lithography—the process of printing circuit patterns onto silicon. As manufacturing nodes advance, lithography becomes more expensive and more energy-intensive. The relevant question is not only whether a 2 nm chip can be produced, but whether society can sustain the power and environmental costs. That is a practical limit distinct from textbook physics alone.

Economics

Deloitte’s 2025 Global Semiconductor Industry Outlook describes market trends: semiconductors remain highly valuable, demand has risen with artificial intelligence (AI) applications, and the industry faces economic stress. Progress depends on funding and market conditions, not only laboratory results.

Geopolitics and equipment supply chains

Hammer (2020), in a chapter from the Center for Strategic and International Studies (CSIS), treats limits partly as a question of who controls manufacturing equipment. With EUV and other advanced tools, leadership involves the supply chain for fabrication equipment, not only chip design. The bottleneck can be access to tools, not only atomic-scale physics on silicon.

Extreme ultraviolet (EUV) as a near-term bottleneck

Among the clearest limits at present is the complexity of cutting-edge equipment, especially EUV lithography systems. Physical effects—heat, leakage, interconnect delay—remain important, but the EUV story connects Hammer (control of tools), Gallagher et al. (cost and energy of lithography), and Deloitte (who can finance new fabrication plants, or fabs). Understanding limits therefore requires both science and institutions.
